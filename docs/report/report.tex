\begin{abstract}
Long-range infrared UAVs can occupy only a few pixels. PinP2-YOLO adapts YOLO26n to this setting by combining shallow pinwheel convolution (PConv) with stride-4 P2 fusion. On the cleaned SIRST-UAVB validation split, PConv alone reduces native AP50:95 by 3.571 percentage points relative to YOLO26n, P2 alone increases it by 2.023 points, and the combined model increases it by 3.322 points. We measure deployment on Raspberry Pi 5 with Hailo-10H using the same 500 validation images. In the original no-NMS pipeline, compared with YOLO26n on this device, PinP2-YOLO improves deployment AP50:95 from 15.204\% to 24.351\% and lowers the miss rate from 35.391\% to 33.539\%, while pipeline throughput falls from 30.06 to 22.72 FPS. We select NMS IoU 0.4 as the deployment default: AP50:95 reaches 31.740\% without changing fixed-threshold recall, while hardware runs achieve 22.89 FPS with a mean NMS cost of 26.3 microseconds per image. A candidate-level vision-language verifier loses recall and does not outperform confidence filtering.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
At long range, an infrared UAV may cover only a few pixels. Clouds and building edges can compete with this weak signal, motivating methods that use local context and shape cues\cite{dai2021acm,zhang2022isnet,li2023dnanet}. Localization is also sensitive to small coordinate errors because even a slight displacement can substantially change the intersection-over-union (IoU) of a small box\cite{wang2021nwd}.

The work spans three big-data features: veracity dominates data preparation, since 494 duplicate images and seven label conflicts were removed from the 3,000-image source; volume drives the training-fraction and workload studies (500, 1,000 and 1,999 training images, and 125, 250 and 500 accelerator images); and velocity decides deployment, where processing frames beside the sensor avoids streaming them, while a finer feature grid raises throughput and memory cost that parameter count alone does not predict.

YOLO predicts locations and scores in one pass\cite{redmon2016yolo}, and YOLO26 uses an end-to-end head without distribution focal loss (DFL)\cite{jocher2026yolo26,ultralytics2026docs}. The finest prediction level in standard YOLO26n has stride 8, leaving the head with a coarse spatial grid. We ask whether directional sampling before fusion and a stride-4 prediction level improve detection, and how the resulting detector performs after accelerator conversion.

PinP2-YOLO (\textit{Pinwheel-enhanced P2 Fusion YOLO}) replaces one shallow downsampling convolution with pinwheel convolution (PConv)\cite{yang2025pconv} and extends the fusion pyramid to stride 4. The new path combines shallow features with context from coarser levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}).

This study contributes a YOLO26n structural adaptation for infrared small targets and an on-device evaluation of that adaptation on Raspberry Pi 5 with Hailo-10H. Four ablation configurations test PConv and P2 individually and together. Comparisons with other detectors on the fixed validation split report AP, miss rate, throughput, and memory, since parameter count alone poorly predicts inference cost\cite{ma2018shufflenet}.

\section{Related Work}
\IEEEpubidadjcol
\subsection{Infrared Small-Target Representation}
Infrared small-target work starts from the observation that a target too small to describe internally can still be located from its surroundings. ACM exchanges high-level semantics with low-level detail through asymmetric contextual modulation\cite{dai2021acm}, DNANet repeats feature interaction through dense nested attention\cite{li2023dnanet}, and ISNet adds shape modelling with two-orientation attention\cite{zhang2022isnet}. These designs motivate the shallow-layer changes in the detector described below.

The original PConv work combines directional convolution with a scale-based dynamic loss\cite{yang2025pconv}. We introduce PConv at one shallow YOLO26n layer and include PConv + SD on YOLOv8n-P2 as another comparison model. A related line replaces IoU with normalized Wasserstein distance to soften tiny-box localization\cite{wang2021nwd}; this work informs our error analysis, while training and evaluation keep IoU.

\subsection{Detection and Multiscale Fusion}
Detection architectures differ in how they route information across scales. YOLO established direct image-to-box prediction\cite{redmon2016yolo}, and YOLOv8n, YOLO11n, and YOLO26 remain the real-time reference\cite{ultralyticscode,jocher2026yolo26}. RT-DETR pairs query selection with a hybrid encoder\cite{zhao2024rtdetr}; PF-RPN studies proposals that adapt to the image through self-prompting\cite{tang2026pfrpn,pfrpncode}. Inspired by PF-RPN, SIA adds image-conditioned sparse attention to the YOLO neck, and SIA + CSP adds cascaded self-prompt updates.

Feature-pyramid design follows the same principle. FPN propagates semantics through lateral connections\cite{lin2017fpn}, PANet strengthens the reverse flow of localization detail\cite{liu2018panet}, and EfficientDet studies weighted bidirectional fusion with joint scaling\cite{tan2020efficientdet}. PinP2-YOLO keeps that principle with concatenation and C3k2 refinement; its extra P2 branch predicts at stride 4, so the finest level no longer depends entirely on stride 8.

\subsection{Efficient Architectures and Edge Inference}
For a camera node, the binding resources are sustained throughput and memory, and efficiency work offers two routes to lower both. MobileNetV2 builds on inverted residuals and linear bottlenecks\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, and GhostNet generates additional feature maps through inexpensive operations\cite{han2020ghostnet}; the available MSF-GhostNet-III implementation serves as a comparison\cite{msfcode}. Both reduce arithmetic, while latency and memory also depend on memory access and platform characteristics\cite{ma2018shufflenet}; this study therefore measures the target devices directly.

Quantization to integer arithmetic lowers both computation and storage, and its accuracy cost depends on the model\cite{jacob2018quantization}. Edge infrared-UAV studies report detection quality under device constraints\cite{auvdsource}; we report the same for H10.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Architecture and Design Rationale}
The first stride-4 stage of YOLO26n uses a standard $3\times3$ downsampling convolution. PinP2-YOLO replaces this layer with PConv and routes the resulting shallow features into a four-level fusion pyramid. PConv samples asymmetric neighborhoods, while the P2 path gives the detection head access to the finer grid.

Let $I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times3}$ be the letterboxed input and $B_l$ the backbone feature at stride $2^l$. The network has 30 top-level modules. PConv maps a $320\times320\times16$ tensor to $160\times160\times32$; for a $640\times640$ input, layers 19, 22, 25, and 28 feed detection at 160, 80, 40, and 20. C3k2 blocks, SPPF pooling, C2PSA attention, and the DFL-free head are unchanged. In Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, parenthesized numbers identify source layers; C and Up denote concatenation and nearest-neighbor upsampling. Dimensions are height $\times$ width $\times$ channels, and the four Detect outputs belong to one multiscale head.

\subsection{Directional Pinwheel Convolution}
For $X\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_{\rm in}}$, define $\mathcal P_{l,r,t,b}$ as zero-padding on the four named sides, and let $\phi_h$ be a $1\times k$ convolution and $\phi_v$ a $k\times1$ convolution, both at stride $s$ with $C_{\rm out}/4$ output channels; each convolution includes batch normalization\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm} and SiLU. With $k=3$ and $s=2$, four branches each convolve a differently padded copy of the input, so that one branch sees the left neighbor, another the right, and likewise above and below:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Z_1&=\phi_h(\mathcal P_{k,0,1,0}(X)),\\
Z_2&=\phi_h(\mathcal P_{0,k,0,1}(X)),\\
Z_3&=\phi_v(\mathcal P_{0,1,k,0}(X)),\\
Z_4&=\phi_v(\mathcal P_{1,0,0,k}(X)).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pconvbranches}
\end{equation}
The horizontal pair shares weights, as does the vertical pair, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}(a). The concatenated responses are fused by a valid $2\times2$ convolution:
\begin{equation}
Y=\phi_{2\times2,s=1}\!\left(\operatorname{Concat}(Z_1,Z_2,Z_3,Z_4)\right).
\label{eq:pconvfuse}
\end{equation}
For even $H,W$, each branch has size $(H/2+1)\times(W/2+1)$, and the final convolution returns $H/2\times W/2$. The operator therefore preserves the downsampling rate while collecting evidence from asymmetric neighborhoods.

Because the convolutions carry no bias and normalization supplies the affine terms, the parameter count follows directly:
\begin{equation}
N_{\rm PC}=\tfrac{k}{2}C_{\rm in}C_{\rm out}+4C_{\rm out}^{2}+3C_{\rm out}.
\label{eq:pcparams}
\end{equation}
These terms count the shared directional kernels, the $2\times2$ fusion, and the normalization scales and offsets. With $C_{\rm in}=16$ and $C_{\rm out}=32$, PConv has 4,960 parameters against 4,672 for the $3\times3$ convolution and normalization it replaces, an increase of 288.

\subsection{High-Resolution Bidirectional Fusion}
Fusion follows the usual two-stage form, with one level added at the bottom. Let $\mathcal U$ be nearest-neighbor upsampling by two, $[\cdot,\cdot]$ channel concatenation, and $\mathcal C_l$ a C3k2 refinement. The top-down hierarchy is
\begin{equation}
T_5=B_5,\qquad T_l=\mathcal C_l([B_l,\mathcal U(T_{l+1})]),\ l=4,3,2.
\label{eq:topdown}
\end{equation}
The added $l=2$ path concatenates $B_2$ with the upsampled $T_3$, producing the stride-4 feature $N_2=T_2$; the concatenation yields 128 channels, which C3k2 reduces to 32. Shallow detail thus enters the fusion directly rather than only through upsampled coarser features.

Let $\mathcal D_l$ be a stride-2 $3\times3$ convolution and $\mathcal R_l$ a C3k2 refinement. The bottom-up path returns this detail to coarser scales:
\begin{equation}
N_l=\mathcal R_l([\mathcal D_l(N_{l-1}),S_l]),\qquad l=3,4,5,
\label{eq:bottomup}
\end{equation}
where $S_3=T_3$, $S_4=T_4$, and $S_5=B_5$. Detection is then performed on all four levels:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathcal B}=\operatorname{Head}(N_2,N_3,N_4,N_5).
\label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
The resulting feature sizes are $160^2\times32$, $80^2\times64$, $40^2\times128$, and $20^2\times256$. At equal channel width, the stride-4 map has four times as many spatial cells as stride 8, so this level increases activation storage and can raise latency even when the parameter count barely moves.

\subsection{Training Objective and Deployment}
PinP2-YOLO retains the YOLO26 training objective and its two assignment branches. For either assignment branch $h$, the criterion combines localization, classification, and normalized box-distance regression:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_h=\lambda_b\mathcal L_{\rm CIoU,h}+\lambda_c\mathcal L_{\rm BCE,h}+\lambda_1\mathcal L_{1,h}.
\label{eq:lossbranch}
\end{equation}
The foreground localization term uses CIoU\cite{zheng2020diou}, whose geometric penalty is
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\rm CIoU}=1-\operatorname{IoU}+\frac{\rho^2(\mathbf c,\mathbf c^*)}{d^2}+\alpha v.
\label{eq:ciou}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf c,\mathbf c^*$ are box centers and $d$ is the smallest enclosing-box diagonal. The aspect-ratio term is $v=4(\arctan(w^*/h^*)-\arctan(w/h))^2/\pi^2$, with $\alpha=v/(1-\operatorname{IoU}+v+\epsilon)$. Classification applies BCE to the assigned target scores, and the L1 term regresses image-normalized left, top, right, and bottom distances. Losses are normalized by assigned score mass. With \texttt{reg\_max=1}, the retained distance term is L1 rather than DFL.

The two assignment branches are combined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=\eta_e\mathcal L_{\rm o2m}+(1-\eta_e)\mathcal L_{\rm o2o}.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where the epoch-dependent $\eta_e$ and the loss gains follow the retained YOLO26 implementation. SD loss appears only in the separate PConv + SD comparator, and PinP2-YOLO keeps the baseline supervision.

Each exported graph is checked before timing: it must reproduce the raw one-to-one branch on eight images within absolute and relative tolerances of $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ and return the same confidence-0.25 detection sets. GPU and Pi CPU share one FP32 path; H10 uses training-only calibration, device inference, and CPU decoding. Backbone arithmetic is INT8, designated raw outputs use a16\_w16 mixed precision, and conversion accuracy is evaluated separately\cite{jacob2018quantization}.

\section{Datasets and Experimental Settings}
\subsection{SIRST-UAVB and the AUVD Transfer Case}
SIRST-UAVB provides 3,000 real infrared images of small UAVs, annotated for UAVs and birds\cite{yang2025pconv}. Image/label, class, coordinate, and pixel-duplicate checks remove 494 duplicates and isolate seven conflicts. The resulting seed-42 split, grouped by inferred background view, contains 1,999 training images with 1,980 UAV boxes and 500 validation images with 486 boxes; 20 training and 15 validation images contain no UAV. UAV is the only positive class. Figure~\ref{fig:training} shows four training examples, including a bird confuser and a UAV-negative scene. Dashed blue/purple boxes indicate UAV/bird annotations; birds serve as confusers in the error analysis and are never trained as a positive UAV class. Insets show the marked $64\times64$-pixel regions without contrast adjustment.

All comparisons use the same frozen manifests and resize and letterbox images to $640\times640$. This split is used for internal validation and checkpoint selection.

The external transfer case uses AUVD-Seg300 (300 images)\cite{auvdsource}. Its segmentation polygons become enclosing boxes after a duplicate screen. The frozen SIRST-trained weights are evaluated on each external image once, with no external finetuning and with the internal thresholds.

\subsection{Baselines, Initialization, and Hardware}
The main comparison contains 11 models, all randomly initialized and trained for 100 epochs: four YOLO26 ablations, YOLO11n and YOLOv8n, PConv + SD / YOLOv8n-P2, MSF-GhostNet-III, RT-DETR-L, and two SIA/CSP adaptations. COCO-initialized and scratch runs trained for 150 epochs form the historical comparison groups.

The YOLO26 family uses seed 42, FP32, AdamW with learning rate 0.001 and final factor 0.01, weight decay 0.0005, momentum configuration 0.9, and nominal batch 32. Given GPU memory limits, the baseline and PConv use physical batch 32, while P2 and PinP2-YOLO use physical batch 8. The other families retain their documented settings.

Training and GPU inference run on an RTX 2080 Ti; Pi CPU uses ONNX Runtime 1.23.2 with four threads, and Hailo-10H uses HailoRT 5.1.1. Seventeen current and historical weights have GPU and Pi CPU measurements; the two additional training-fraction weights have GPU measurements. Seven current models complete 20 warmups plus 500 H10 inferences. SIA, SIA + CSP, and MSF failed raw-head verification, while RT-DETR-L has no H10 measurement in this study. Accelerator timing excludes CPU fallback.

H10 compilation uses 1,024 seed-42 training images; SDK logs record 64 images used for calibration statistics. Calibration draws on training images only, with finetuning, QAT, and Adaround switched off. Conversion and hardware timing run sequentially for the evaluated models.

Metric definitions: $\operatorname{IoU}(b,g)=|b\cap g|/|b\cup g|$, $P=TP/(TP+FP)$, $R=TP/(TP+FN)$, $F_1=2PR/(P+R)$, and missed-object rate $M=FN/(TP+FN)$.

AP uses confidence floor 0.001 and trapezoidal integration of a 101-point precision envelope. For class $c$ and threshold $\tau$, $\operatorname{AP}_{c,\tau}=\int_0^1p^{\rm interp}_{c,\tau}(r)\,dr$, with $\operatorname{mAP}_{50}=\operatorname{AP}_{c,0.5}$ and $\operatorname{mAP}_{50:95}$ the mean of $\operatorname{AP}_{c,\tau}$ over $\tau=0.50{:}0.05{:}0.95$ for the single class $C=1$. With $t_{50}$ and $t_{95}$ the empirical median and 95th-percentile latency, $\operatorname{FPS}=1000/\bar t$.

Native metrics use the best AP50:95 checkpoint. YOLO26 native P/R uses the evaluator's maximum-smoothed-F1 point, while deployment P/R uses confidence 0.25. Deployment metrics are recomputed from saved predictions.

Model size counts unique native-graph tensors, including normalization parameters and excluding running buffers, with shared tensors counted once; exported file sizes are reported in MiB from the file bytes. GPU timing is synchronized across runs. Pipeline time includes preprocessing, device transfer, model execution, and decoding and filtering, and excludes drawing, encoding, network transfer, and display. The original YOLO26 end-to-end route keeps the top 300 predictions without NMS, so its recorded NMS-IoU setting is inactive. The YOLOv8/YOLO11 routes apply NMS at IoU 0.7. The supplementary NMS replay below is measured and reported on its own. RSS reports process memory.

\subsection{Workload and Error-Stratification Protocol}
Final-model scaling measures inference workloads using the same frozen model weights. A seed-42 permutation defines nested sets of 125, 250, and 500 validation images, and each size is measured three times in a fresh process, using size orders (125,250,500), (250,500,125), and (500,125,250). Every process checks that it loads the same HEF and data, uses batch 1, performs 20 warmups, and releases the shared device on completion. Timing covers image read and decode, preprocessing, synchronous H10 inference, host decoding, filtering, and prediction recording, and excludes initialization, warmups, metric scoring, result compression, drawing, and network access. Normal filesystem caching stays on, and AP is computed in canonical manifest order so that equal scores sort reproducibly.

Error strata are fixed in advance. Object size is $d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)(y_2-y_1)}$ in original-image pixels, binned as $d<8$, $8\leq d<16$, and $d\geq16$. Background texture is measured on normalized $256\times256$ grayscale images:
\begin{equation}
C(I)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{p\in\Omega}\sqrt{(\partial_x I(p))^2+(\partial_y I(p))^2}.
\label{eq:clutter}
\end{equation}
Here $\partial_x,\partial_y$ are central differences, and $\Omega$ excludes UAV and bird boxes expanded by four original pixels. The low, medium, and high strata use terciles from the fixed 1,024 calibration-source training images, with thresholds of 0.011011 and 0.015144. Original bird boxes are recovered by image-file identity and UAV-label checks. After UAV matching, remaining predictions are matched one-to-one to bird annotations at IoU $\geq0.5$, and these matches count as UAV false alarms attributable to annotated birds.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the Extension Improves}
P2 improves native AP50:95 with or without PConv; PConv alone reduces AP, while its addition to P2 gives a smaller gain. The combined model trades throughput for higher H10 AP and fewer misses than YOLO26n. Against YOLO11n and YOLOv8n, it uses fewer parameters and runs faster, with the accuracy tradeoffs in Table~\ref{tab:edge}.

\subsection{Remaining Errors and Deployment Cost}
NMS leaves the 163 misses (153 below eight pixels) and the 20 bird-associated false positives untouched. Default IoU 0.4 is set on this validation set; an independent review, especially of closely spaced targets in continuous video, tests its operating margin. P2 also increases Pi CPU memory, and host processing contributes substantial latency (Table~\ref{tab:edge}).

\subsection{Validity and Generalization}
AUVD is a single external source, so broad generalization and independent video remain open questions. The ablation draws on one seed and differs in physical batch, so its stability across seeds is untested; hardware repeats add timing statistics only, not accuracy samples.

\section{Conclusion}
The extension, the 11-model comparison, the three training sizes and the edge deployment were completed. Four changes remain: scratch 100-epoch training on SIRST-UAVB alone, a single ablation seed, one PConv position, and no independent video evaluation.
PinP2-YOLO improves native AP50:95 by 3.322 points over YOLO26n in the scratch100 ablation. Its H10 deployment trades speed for accuracy; default NMS IoU 0.4 reaches 31.740\% AP50:95 and reduces FP119 to FP62 at unchanged recall and 22.89 benchmark FPS. The tested VLM policy offers no advantage over confidence filtering.

\section{Personal Contribution and Reflection}
\subsection{Yaorong Huang}
My work covered the model, the experiments and the edge deployment of PinP2-YOLO: the shallow PConv and stride-4 P2 structure with its network drawing, all comparison and ablation training under one configuration, ONNX export and Hailo-10H testing on Raspberry Pi 5, the NMS audit of the end-to-end head, a negative VLM review, and the report and reproducibility material.

The largest difficulty was that the network rebuilt by the training pipeline, and therefore trained, might not be the designed one: my custom PConv and P2 modules were dropped or reverted to native layers on rebuild. Temporary patches could not keep training, export and deployment on the same path. I moved the modules into a version-pinned adapter layer that restores them, checked module types and parameter counts after every rebuild, and compared outputs across the three stages.

PConv alone lowered accuracy and only the PConv-P2 combination improved it, so a change can be judged only by factor ablation. The gain came from better localization while small targets were still missed, and a single seed, unequal ablation batches and a threshold chosen on the same validation set limit the conclusions.

\subsection{Chunshen Yu}
I prepared the slides and result visualisations: the thirteen-slide offline HTML presentation with its charts, the speaker notes and a backup question-and-answer page, and an automatic deployment linked from the project README.

The biggest difficulty was keeping the visual design consistent with the data. Early candidate charts placed results from different training protocols, devices or thresholds side by side, which implied comparisons the experiments do not support. Layout decides which results an audience treats as comparable. I revised slides more often than necessary early on, and the deck still needs a table-by-table check against the final report.

\subsection{Yifan Zhai}
I led the application testing and error analysis: I ran the released detector on practical scenes rather than only on the validation set, sorted the failures by background complexity, target size and confidence threshold, and proofread the report and corrected its wording.

One observed failure had several plausible causes, since a missed target can come from a strong background, small size or an unsuitable threshold. I kept the tests separate, changed one factor at a time, and recorded the scene and threshold behind every failure.

Error analysis is more useful than a single accuracy figure, but it must stay tied to visible examples. This work would be stronger with an agreed failure-taxonomy sheet shared across the team before the tests began.
\subsection{Zhiwen Luo}
I designed and built the dataset: I gathered and cleaned the SIRST-UAVB images and labels, removed duplicates, isolated conflicting annotations, fixed the training and validation split, kept the data under version control, and wrote the dataset documentation.

The raw pool was not clean: duplicated images reappear under different file names and some annotations disagree. I audited by image content and source group rather than by file name, quarantined conflicts instead of deleting them, and froze the split so later code could not move images between training and validation.

Data work is mostly bookkeeping, and an error there is invisible in the results. My main doubt is scale: a small validation set makes most differences coarse, and one duplicated sample can inflate its accuracy more than extra training data could correct.
